**Guide:**

The public reaction to Olmstead crystallizes a choice that the culture now offers instead of a single verdict: the audience can decide the paintings are not art, that a child produced them and therefore skill was never the issue, or that the whole affair was staged by adults to inflate value. None of these possibilities generates outrage on the order of a riot. They generate suspicion, amusement, argument in comment sections and living rooms—registers considerably cooler than fury. That coolness is itself the datum. Cultural shocks by the twenty-first century have become mild almost by definition; the available responses multiply while their intensity diminishes.

The next turn sharpens why. Through the first half of the twentieth century, art could still shock through form alone—Duchamp did not need obscene content, only the claim that a mass-produced urinal, once signed, qualified as art, and that claim alone was sufficient to violate expectations. The 1960s and 1970s mark the point past which form stops being able to do that work. Andy Warhol's repeated Marilyns and his Brillo boxes unsettled parts of the art community but never produced anything like public outrage; the wider public response was closer to bewilderment or indifference than offense.

The clearest instance of that shift is John Cage's Water Walk, performed in 1960 not in a gallery or concert hall but on the television game show I've Got a Secret. Cage moved around the stage producing sounds with a water pitcher, a rubber duck, and other ordinary objects, while host Garry Moore introduced him with a mixture of respect and skepticism, and the studio audience occasionally laughed but never rioted. Forty-five years after Zurich, the confrontation between avant-garde and popular audience had become polite.
