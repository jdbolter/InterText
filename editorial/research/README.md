# Art-reception research cases

`art-reception-cases.json` is a working research dataset for thinking about the contrast between early avant-garde and later receptions of art. It is **not** a statistically representative sample, a section package, or material already accepted into the editorial fund. It is not loaded by the guide or shown in the editorial preview.

## The question these cases serve

The author's question is not whether one early audience reacted more loudly than one later audience. It is whether the historical avant-garde and its opponents operated in a culture that took art *as art* more seriously: its forms, boundaries, and cultural authority seemed consequential enough to fight over. Section 5's existing spine already puts this most directly in passage 3: the avant-garde and its detractors treated “serious” art as worth fighting over. The records below are possible evidence for examining that larger attitude, not fifteen separate claims about how an entire period felt.

“More seriously” does not mean uniformly reverently, intelligently, or sympathetically. Ridicule, rejection, and deliberate provocation may also show that participants thought the category of art mattered. Nor does it mean that contemporary people have ceased to care deeply about art. The working comparison concerns the *kind and reach of the stakes*: whether a dispute over an artwork's form or status could plausibly invoke a broadly shared cultural standard, as distinct from disputes centered on particular artistic communities, public funding, representation, religion, or market value. These categories can overlap in either period. The present cases can test and complicate this interpretation; by themselves they cannot establish a general historical decline.

Each record separates the documented reaction from a possible interpretation and a qualification. This matters because press ridicule, a divided premiere audience, institutional exclusion, legal categorization, political sanction, and market absorption are different kinds of evidence. A famous scandal does not by itself prove a period-wide historical trend. Similarly, a later protest does not by itself erase a change in the cultural authority attached to art's own categories.

The 1913 Armory Show is represented by separate New York and Chicago reception records because the Chicago student protest is a distinct, well-documented episode. The *Rite of Spring* record deliberately describes the divided disruption without treating every detail of the familiar “riot” story as settled. The Russolo record is a provisional lead because the dramatic accounts are partly partisan. The *L'Âge d'or* case shows that early avant-garde conflict could already be driven by religion and politics, not only formal experiment. Later cases are comparators, not a claim that audiences or institutions can be matched one-to-one across eras.

Before converting any record into a fund entry or authored detour, decide what question it answers, check its source claims more deeply, and determine whether it belongs in the spine, optional evidence, or an alternative inquiry path. The cited institutions sometimes participated in the events they describe; `sourceType` and `qualification` make that visible.

For further research, do not just collect more scandals. Look for broader evidence of what different publics expected art to do and who claimed authority to judge it: contemporary criticism and letters, attendance and discussion, institutional decisions, education, patronage, and comparable later records. For each case, ask who responded, what they thought was at stake, whether their response concerned art's status or another value, and how far that view plausibly reached beyond the immediate group. Preserve contrary evidence and avoid turning a memorable episode into a proxy for public opinion.
