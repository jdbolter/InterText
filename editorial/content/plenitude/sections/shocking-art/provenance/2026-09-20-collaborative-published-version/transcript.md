**Guide:**

The category now called "art" is not a natural kind but a historical invention. Larry Shiner traced this in *The Invention of Art* (2001): before the eighteenth century, no line reliably separated the arts from the crafts. art became a special and singularly important form of making in the eighteenth century. Prior to this time, there had been no universally held distinction between arts and crafts. Painters, poets, jewelers, and woodworkers all practiced callings that were not sorted into "fine" and "merely useful" categories.

The invention itself did specific ideological work. After the invention of art, the fine arts became a matter of inspiration and genius, meant to be enjoyed for themselves in moments of refined pleasure, whereas the crafts and popular arts required only skill and rules and were meant for mere use or entertainment. That division was not a discovery about the nature of creative work; it was a social sorting mechanism, and one that persisted with remarkable stability. In the first half of the twentieth century, art still enjoyed this remarkable status. The educated and moneyed elites were able to define what art was, and their definition was deemed to have value for the whole of our culture. Working people had little direct access to elite art but were still expected to defer to its cultural authority.

That deference is precisely what has evaporated. The Kinkade case is not an isolated embarrassment for the art world — it is a symptom of the fact that the mechanism which once manufactured universal consent to a definition no longer functions. The next question is what took its place.
