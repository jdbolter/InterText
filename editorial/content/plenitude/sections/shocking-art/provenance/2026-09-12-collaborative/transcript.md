**Guide:**

The correction is right, and it sharpens rather than complicates the claim being made. Futurism was not merely negational — Marinetti's movement proposed a violently affirmative program: worship of speed, machinery, war as what he called the world's only hygiene, a rejection of museums and moonlight in favor of factories and combustion engines. Dada, by contrast, arrived out of the catastrophe of the First World War with something closer to pure negation, treating even Futurism's affirmations as complicit in the violence that had just discredited European civilization. Tzara's own manifestos insist on Dada meaning nothing, deliberately refusing the kind of positive replacement-program Futurism offered.

What the two share, then, is not a single negative aim but a common diagnosis: inherited forms — the sonnet, the symphony, the well-made play, the framed representational canvas — no longer answered to the conditions of the world producing them. Futurism responded by proposing new forms adequate to speed and mechanization; Dada responded by refusing the premise that any coherent new form was possible after 1914. Both used provocation, parody, and formal rupture as instruments to force that inadequacy into the open, rather than to argue it discursively.

As for the Armory Show: critics did not merely register bafflement in general terms. One widely repeated response described Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase* as resembling "an explosion in a shingle factory" — a phrase itself signaling ridicule rather than considered rejection. That specificity matters more than a generic verdict of alarm, because it shows the public still treating the violation as worth mocking in detail, which is itself a form of the shared hierarchy under discussion.
