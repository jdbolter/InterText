## Shocking Art

Our culture's reaction to art has changed and in general diminished. Because there is no elite whose position on art validates its significance for the rest of us, there is much less at stake. Various art communities still fight over the work produced by their members, but outsiders are neither interested nor in general threatened. For the general public, the work of professional artists in the traditional sense is sometimes engaging, sometimes tasteless or incomprehensible, but it is not dangerous. The absence of shock value shows the position of art has changed in the past century.

In the first decades of the twentieth century, the Futurists and Dadaists set out to demonstrate the emptiness of the art of their day. They made noise and called it music; they performed plays that lasted three minutes and had no resolution; they pulled words clipped from newspaper articles out of a hat and called them poems. In *Dada, Art and Anti-Art* (1978), Hans Richter described, for example, what he called the climax of Dada activity in Zurich: a performance in a large hall in April of 1919, in which a host of Dada figures appeared on stage one after another. One delivered a lecture on abstract art; another danced to the atonal music of Arnold Schönberg. Tristan Tzara addressed and insulted the audience. Finally when Walter Serner started to read his manifesto...

> "the young men, most of whom were in the gallery, leaped on to the stage, brandishing pieces of the balustrade... chased Serner into the wings and out of the building, smashed the tailor's dummy [his prop], and stamped on the bouquet. The whole place was in an uproar." (78-79)

It seems clear from Richter's description that the audience itself was ready to be provoked, looking to become part of the performance. And the anti-art of the Dadaists delivered: it was meant to shock the bourgeois, to offend their sensibilities by insisting that their traditions of poetry, dance, and drama were meaningless.

Throughout this period in Europe and North America, the shocks continued. The New York Armory Show of 1913 was meant to introduce the European avant-garde to the United States, and the critics reacted appropriately. The Surrealists continued to promote art as scandal in the 1920s: their best-known example was probably Buñuel's film *Un Chien Andalou* (1929). When Marcel Duchamp submitted *Fountain*, his famous signed urinal, to an exhibition of the Society of Independent Artists, he was apparently pleased that he had anointed as art an object that even his avant-garde colleagues could not accept. In a sense the negative reaction to the historical avant-garde only confirmed the importance of art; it showed that they and their detractors still took "serious" art seriously, as something worth fighting over.

There were avant-gardes all through the twentieth century, and most of the time, they were simply ignored, certainly by the public and sometimes by the elite art community as a whole. The most compelling of the avant-gardes, such as Cubism and Surrealism, became influential in mainstream elite art and eventually commercial art as well. In the 1950s, the American Abstract Expressionists managed to be at least mildly shocking and mainstream almost in the same moment. The reception for Pollock and other American expressionists indicated the changes that were already occurring in American elite culture in the 1950s. Pollock might be a great artist or not, but few in the art community could claim that his paintings were not art simply because they did not represent anything. They would also have to deny a whole strain of modern art from Mondrian to Klee to Kandinsky. The public outside that community, however, still expected a painting to look like something. Although no longer shocked enough to throw fruit, the public in the 1950s might have the reaction that Pollock was not art because it apparently required no skill to produce. Anyone could drip paint on canvas. Art critics like Clement Greenberg, on the other hand, analyzed the skill with which Pollock chose his strokes and his colors.

The reaction that Abstract Expressionism requires no skill and is therefore not art remains common today, as was illustrated by the documentary *My Kid Could Paint That* (Bar-Lev, 2007)---a film that captures the ambivalences of our contemporary attitude toward art. Four-year-old Marla Olmstead from Binghamton, New York produced abstract paintings of such apparent quality that they attracted the attention of the art community and the market. Some of her paintings sold for thousands to rich collectors. A segment on the television show *60 Minutes II* (July, 2005) presented Marla as a prodigy, but with the lingering doubt: is there really any aesthetic difference between a Pollock and the smearings of a four-year-old? The reporting team then took the story in another direction; they began to suggest that Marla may have had help in producing the paintings.

On the one hand, the paintings may not be art. On the other, perhaps abstract painting is art after all and requires the sophistication of an adult artist. The audience has a choice of shocks: that this is art, that a child could produce it, or that the whole thing is a fraud, perpetrated by adults to raise the value of the paintings. Such cultural shocks are mild in the twenty-first century.

In the first half of the twentieth century, art could indeed be shocking through its very form: for example, insisting that a mass-produced urinal was art because an artist had signed it. The 1960s and 1970s marked a turning point, because from this time on, art could no longer be a danger to the order of things simply because it abuses the traditional materials and forms of art. Andy Warhol's repeated images of Marilyn Monroe or his Brillo boxes upset many in the art community in the 1960s, but they never led to public outrage. The more general reaction to the avant-garde in this period was bewilderment and disinterest. In 1960 the artist-musician John Cage created a piece that he performed with various objects such as a water pitcher and a rubber duck. His *Water Walk* was like other avant-garde performances of the period, except that it was performed on the television game show *I've Got a Secret*. Cage's secret was that he was a composer. The host, Gary Moore, explained to an unsuspecting audience that they were about to hear something unusual, and his interview with Cage was both respectful and skeptical. Cage then performed for about 5 minutes by moving around on the stage producing the appropriate sounds with the various objects, while the audience listened and occasionally laughed. It was a bizarre confrontation between the avant-garde and popular culture, and the audience's reactions and the way the whole program was presented showed how the assumptions of elite art were breaking down. The audience did not storm the stage, as audiences had done 45 years earlier when the Dadaists performed. They were more polite, perhaps not sure that elite art should still be respected, but also doubtful that this *was* elite art. Within the art and music communities, Cage's work was both controversial and highly influential. But for the public, any changes in the form and materials for making music were no longer dangerous or shocking.

From the 1960s on, because artists could no longer rely on formal innovation to shock their audience, they had to resort to nudity, bodily disfiguration, threatened violence, and so on. The French artist Yves Klein used naked models covered in blue paint as "living brushes"; he is also seen in a photomontage that appears to show him leaping arms outstretched from a balcony onto the street below. Performance and body artists (such as Carolee Schneeman and Chris Burden) became famous for dangerous or explicit acts of self exposure or even mutilation. Stelarc's various performances include piercing his own body and attaching a robotic third arm. Within the art community, the work of performance and installation artists led to a dialogue about the political and formal meaning of art in the collapse of modernism. The millions outside of that community, of course, do not participate in that dialogue. They may be provoked to deny that this is art, not only because such performances do not require the skills expected of a traditional painter, sculptor, or even stage actor, but also because they judge the content itself to be indecent or violent.

In the United States, the shock can be dangerous when it touches on religion, especially when it questions Christian beliefs or the tenets of patriotism as a kind of American religion. Ultimately, the combination of perceived obscenity and sacrilege put an end to the federal funding of experimental art. In 1987, for example, Andres Serrano created *Piss Christ*, which enraged conservative American politicians. The response to Serrano's work was so strong not because he was profaning art, but because he was blaspheming religion and using public dollars to do so. He had received a small amount of support from the National Endowment for the Arts. Citing Serrano, performance artist Karen Finley, and others as examples of inappropriate art, the Republican party worked to defund and eliminate the Endowment in the 1990s. It was the content of all this work, not its form, that provoked and threatened.

Art in the 1990s could continue to shock only by using abject materials or invoking offensive social or religious themes, as did the 1997 exhibit *Sensation*, featuring the Young British Artists. In the United States, in particular, politicians expressed outrage whenever public money or public institutions were involved. But contemporary publics were not so much shocked as confused or simply bored by contemporary art. In 1993 and again in 2012, *60 Minutes* presented a segment in which Morley Safer visited contemporary art exhibitions. Safer came to ask the question that the avant-gardes traditionally wanted posed: is this art? But he affected to be bemused rather than scandalized. In the 2012 broadcast, the only shock comes from the outlandish prices for the work on sale. Safer wanders through the exhibition followed by the curious television camera exploring the strange installations and pieces that are counted as art by the community of artists, dealers and collectors. He and the television audience are clearly visitors, not members of this community.
