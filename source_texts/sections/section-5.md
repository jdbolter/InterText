## The Uncanny is a Feature, not a Bug

This essay, like some others in this collection, argues that CG and VR should be understood as belonging to and remediating the representational practices of live-action film. In his compelling essay opening this section of this volume, however, William Uricchio suggests that VR is antithetical to cinema in its "visual constructions, organization of time, and possibilities of story." Uricchio's insights are valuable, and, we would argue, do not necessarily contradict the quality of CG and VR that most closely connects them to film: their ambivalent and uncanny claim to capture reality. CG and VR's preoccupation with the uncanny valley indicates a similar concern to the one that has defined film as a medium—the concern whether their medium is a mere substitution for the real and the authentic. Yet, film and VR each occupy or have occupied a different position in the contemporary media landscape.

As a new visual medium at the turn of the 20th century, film entered a media landscape that included painting and photography. As a mode of entertainment, it was consumed by audiences in a fashion similar to stage productions including live drama and popular entertainments such as magic shows. Hence economically and culturally film was a rival and potential replacement for these stage events. Ontologically, film competed with painting and photography in representing the real. As a technology of mechanical reproduction, it could claim to be better than painting because it recorded the world without the invention of the artist. It could claim to be better than photography, because it captured the world in motion. But it remained an uncanny substitute for the real. The audience must always be aware at some level that they were watching images projected on a screen, which is the real point of the La Ciotat story. The audience could only be astonished at how much the train on the screen looked like a real train if they knew that it was only a moving image. This uncanny doubling is the essence of film, which helps to explain filmmakers' and audiences' fear and fascination with themes of doubling and substitution.

After more than a century, film today remains an important medium, having successfully negotiated the challenge posed by television in the middle and video games in the second half of the 20th century, and the popularity boom of virtual reality in the 2010s. Videogames and related forms of CG/VR constitute a new challenge to film's (and television's) cultural and ontological status. They make a virtue of the fact that they are not limited to recording the world but that they can immerse their users in an infinite space of imaginary 3D worlds—such as the metaverse— soon to be made even more responsive through techniques of generative AI. At least since the 1990s, mainstream filmmakers have responded to CG/VR and AI with films with anxiety but also fascination, and that fascination has led them to incorporate or imitate digital styles from video games with the result that, as we have noted, film may now often be more post-cinematic than VR itself.

In principle, CG (like cel animation) is freed of the need to faithfully represent the physical world. Yet CG continues to make photorealism one of its key measures and so inherits film's anxiety about representing the real. Uncanny doubling is as much a fascination and concern for video games as it is for traditional film, and many VR experiences and other digital forms that depend on CG continue to pursue realistic representation. The uncanny valley is a term borrowed from robotics, but it also invokes the long tradition of the uncanny in film. Thus the uncanny is as essential to CG/VR as it is to film. Imagine a time in the future when CG/VR developers do manage to achieve perfect photorealism of human figures. Imagine the metaverse in which we as users could interact with NPCs (non-player characters) that were visually indistinguishable from humans and through advances in generative AI, could hold convincing conversations. The uncanny valley would have been crossed, yet these characters and this world would be more uncanny than ever. We would still know that this world was synthetic—that it had a different ontological status than the world that we experience when we take off the headset. The only way that the metaverse could lose the quality of the uncanny would be if human users were to forget the existence of the world outside.

For both film and CG, the uncanny is a feature, not a bug.
