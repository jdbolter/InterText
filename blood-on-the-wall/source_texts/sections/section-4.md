## The Files Break Open

When the East German state finally buckled in 1989, the crowds that breached the Wall in November did not stop at champagne and hammers. They marched on the Stasi's Normannenstraße headquarters demanding the assassination files specifically, tearing open filing cabinets that had been sealed for twenty-six years. What poured out — names, cables, half-answers about how much Ulbricht's superiors had known in 1963 — became the template other post-communist states would later borrow: public reckonings with secret-police archives as the price of legitimacy.

By 1993, reunified Germany maintains a standing Office for Stasi Records with international access unmatched by any other country's archive. Joint American-German counterintelligence units, born from 1960s cooperation, still operate. The assassination site in Berlin carries a permanent security perimeter and a memorial, and its anniversary draws heads of state each June. Yet the reckoning is incomplete: some officers escaped to Moscow or Latin America, the question of what the Kremlin knew remains legally and politically radioactive, and in Russia's own constitutional crisis that autumn, nationalist deputies denounce the whole affair as Western fabrication weaponized against Russian dignity. The Cold War has ended, but its founding wound in Berlin has not closed — only scarred over, publicly, imperfectly, in full view of the cameras.
