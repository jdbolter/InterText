## A Fog of Distrust

Detente, when it came, came late and thin. Nixon's opening to China in 1972 proceeded on its own logic, since Sino-Soviet hostility had its own momentum from the border clashes of 1969. But the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks that same year moved through a fog of distrust no Berlin thaw had ever cleared; SALT I was signed, but each side treated the other's signature as tactical rather than sincere. When the Helsinki Accords were finally negotiated in 1975, Western delegations insisted on attaching explicit grievance language about the Berlin assassination — a demand that nearly wrecked the talks and that gave the human-rights provisions, ordinarily a diplomatic afterthought, a sharpened edge. Dissident networks inside the GDR seized on that language, using Helsinki's promises as license to document Stasi abuses with a specificity aimed directly at the ministry implicated in Kennedy's death.

By the time Ronald Reagan stood before evangelicals in 1983 to call the Soviet Union an evil empire, he had a proof text ready-made: a dead American president, killed by a communist secret police on free soil. Berlin was no longer only a symbol of division; it was Exhibit A in an indictment. The Strategic Defense Initiative and the decade's hardened rhetoric moved through Western publics with unusual unity, because the argument no longer required abstraction. It pointed at a name and a date.
