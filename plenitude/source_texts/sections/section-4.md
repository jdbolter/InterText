## The Case of Music

In the 1950s the Philadelphia Symphony and the other orchestras in the "Big Five" in the United States (and of course the orchestras of Europe) were still flourishing. But the shift in popular music from the big bands and crooners of the pre-war period to jazz and especially to rock-and-roll was changing cultural attitudes towards music in general. Prior to the 1950s, few questioned that the musical tradition from Bach to at least Stravinsky was one of the great achievements of Western culture, incomparably more profound than popular music. Increasingly the popular music of the 1950s and 1960s was made by people barely out of their teens for people still in their teens. And although the music had roots, for example in African-American blues, it owed nothing to the classical tradition. This music posed perhaps the first successful challenge to the hierarchy of the arts in North America and Europe.

The Chuck Berry song "Roll Over Beethoven," performed later by the Beatles and many others, captured perfectly what was happening. The song and youth culture in general did not harbor real hostility to classical music, because it did not know or care about the music. Berry simply used Beethoven as a metaphor for the music of high culture that his music replaces for his youthful audience (Kozinin 2011). Jay Z's song "Picasso Baby" celebrates the culmination of this cultural process. Like Berry, the rapper is telling Picasso to move over, but by 2013, recognized artists are happy to participate in his performance. Jay Z is confident about his status in a way that Berry was not.

In the 1960s, music was one of the cultural activities in which the term "artist" began to be applied more and more widely without irony, until it included all kinds of musical performers. In fact, unlike popular singers, performers of classical music were and still are usually referred to more specifically as "soprano" or "violinist" rather than "artist." In the 1960s, *The Huntley-Brinkley Report*, NBC's nightly news program, used an excerpt from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony as its closing theme. It was a 1950s recording by Toscanini, an iconic figure rather like Stokowski, and the NBC Symphony Orchestra. Today it is unthinkable that any television news show would use a classical music standard as a theme, let alone that an American network would maintain its own symphony orchestra.

Throughout this period, the community of composers, musicians, and audiences who believed in the evident superiority of classical music grew smaller and less convincing. New hybrid figures appeared, such as Brian Eno and David Byrne, whose music, though not part of the classical tradition, was taken seriously as artistic expression. Classically trained composers, among them Philip Glass, crossed over into popular performance with minimalist music. Meanwhile, the conservative symphonic sound was refashioned for large popular audiences in Hollywood film by John Williams, Vangelis, Howard Shore, and many others. Above all, the enormous audiences for rock, country, hip-hop, and various other popular music genres demanded cultural recognition. By the end of the century, American culture had clearly accepted the notion that all the various musical styles or traditions were of equal creative potential. The audience for symphonic classical music had diminished, but not disappeared. Today, even some of the great orchestras in large American cities, which were founded in the nineteenth century in a conscious effort by business philanthropists to bring European culture to the United States (Levine 1988, 104-140), find it difficult to fund their seasons. In Europe, the audiences are more faithful, and there is still significant government support as there is for theater and opera (Judt 2005, 778-779). Opera in both America and Europe survives largely for audiences who may dress up in an effort to recall the era of elite music, and there is at the same time a successful trend to broadcast opera to a secondary audience in film theaters in high-quality stereo. Opera stars and soloists resort to gimmicks (the three-tenor concerts) to generate publicity and larger audiences. Meanwhile, the classical community engages in endless debates on whether and how to make classical music "relevant": to increase the size of its audience, for example, by making the concerts less stodgy (e.g. Wakin 2012). And the term "artist" is used simply to designate the performer column in the iTunes music interface.

In 1913, the première of Stravinsky's dissonant and rhythmically violent ballet *The Rite of Spring* led to a riot. Whether the rioters were in fact incensed by the music, rather than the choreography, it was certainly believable that a Parisian audience could care enough about the fate of music to break into fistfights over an avant-garde piece. It is unthinkable today that any music portrayed as classical could provoke its docile audience to riot. (However, fans can occasionally be moved to violence when a famous rock band cancels a concert.) If the music is not to a listener's taste, she goes elsewhere, for she has an infinite number of musical choices, all available from a variety of sources, especially Internet streaming and download.

The plenitude now accommodates musical communities that intersect, cooperate, compete, and above all ignore each other. The formerly elite classical community is certainly present on the Web. Classical music can be purchased and downloaded on iTunes, and classical music magazines have websites. There are classical music blogs, and some opera stars have their own blogs and fan sites. All the digital and other media forms that support popular music are also available for formerly elite music, although the sites are less visited, the music less often downloaded. The opera diva Anna Netrebko is present, like any rock star, in most of the various digital forms from websites to iTunes to Facebook to YouTube to Twitter, although she will have much smaller numbers in all these forms than a rock star. (For example, she had somewhat more than 50,000 followers on Twitter; Justin Timberlake has over 61.5 million.) She is regarded as a star within her community, but not necessarily across communities. The Internet does not put Netrebko in competition with rock stars, for she remains in a different community. The converging of musical tastes into one community never happened, although it was perhaps closer to happening in the period prior to the Internet when the three television networks in the United States and local radio chains with replicable formats dominated the airwaves. The plenitude creates numerous hierarchies, which can be braided into strands (sharing some common artists or performers) or held quite separate.

This in itself is an important shift from the mid-twentieth century. The great divide between elite and popular music has been replaced with numerous smaller divides and provisional connections. The Wikipedia article lists over 250 "genres" of rock music, each with a link to a descriptive article: including Bandana Thrash (a subgenre of Thrashcore), Krautrock, Swedish Death Metal, and The New Wave of New Wave (Wikipedia, "List of Rock Genres" 2017). The vastly popular hip hop, which began as an African-American subculture and spread to world-wide status, has dozens of genres too: including Avant-garde hip hop, chopper rap, and crunk (Wikipedia, "List of Hip Hop Genres" 2017). We cite these names not to make fun of them, but rather to indicate the intensity of interest in these popular forms. Some of these genres imply not only a particular aesthetic, but also social status. To someone outside of the rock community in general, the differences between Death Metal, Doom Metal, and Industrial Metal may be hardly noticeable, but to those within the community it may be a matter not only of taste, but even political significance. This is certainly true of hip hop, where the international versions may not share the social and political concerns of African-American culture. People do not all enjoy the same music today, and with the technologies of streaming music services, mp3 players, and satellite radio receivers they have infinitely more choices than ever before. These technologies have perhaps made listeners less tolerant of music styles they do not like or understand. Earphones make it easy to create a music bubble almost anywhere and to fill that bubble with one's favorite style.
