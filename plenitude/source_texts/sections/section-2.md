## The Philadelphia (Symphony) Story

In April, 2011 the Philadelphia Symphony orchestra filed for Chapter 11 protection from creditors; it was the first time that one of the "Big Five" American orchestras had gone bankrupt. The orchestra has continued to hold its seasons, unlike many smaller US orchestras, but its bankruptcy was emblematic of the diminishing status of the traditional elite arts. This orchestra once embodied the distinction between high- and low-brow culture. Yet, in 1939, Leopold Stokowski, the orchestra's flamboyant director, directed the music for the Disney film *Fantasia*, in which seven abridged and rearranged pieces from Bach to Stravinsky were played over the animated graphics. The film showed that American popular culture was already confident enough to seek to appropriate high culture, but in a way that still honored the hierarchy of art. Stokowski appeared as a silhouetted figure in the animation, and he was not the butt of jokes. He was treated by Mickey Mouse with nervous reverence: popular culture approached elite culture on the latter's terms. In 1941, that was still not enough for cultural purists. The columnist Dorothy Thompson wrote in the *New York Herald Tribune* that the film is a symptom of "the collapse of the civilized world" (Telotte 2008, 37). Although Disney apparently thought that this marriage of great classical music and great popular animation would inaugurate a new art form, the film was not profitable in its first release, in part because it was considered too high-brow by the traditional audiences for the Disney classics and in part because of the costs of its innovative stereo technology (Telotte 2008, 37-41; Boone 1941).

The introduction of sound in film allowed the preeminent popular entertainment medium to address all kinds of musical cultures. In the United States, opera enjoyed a high status at the beginning of the 20th century; it could be said to represent high art and European culture to American culture at large. That status was, however, already beginning to change by the time of the Great Depression. As a symbol of the upper class and its lack of connection with the economic and social problems of the working classes, opera was open to parody and became the subject of the famous Marx Brothers comedy *A Night at the Opera* in 1935. Yet there remained a feeling that opera was part of a legitimate high culture. The Marx Brothers wrapped their parody of opera and high culture around a romantic plot, and the romantic leads were singers with operatic voices. Their songs, while popular, were lyrical and meant to be appreciated as artful. *Fantasia* showed even greater respect for the classical music that it appropriated. The hierarchy of culture prevailed in the 1930s, even if it was beginning to be undermined by the economic importance and social status of popular music.

The situation was different 20 years later when another animated cartoon appeared: *What's Opera, Doc?* (1957). This was a parody of Wagnerian opera in which Bugs Bunny plays Brünnhilde to Elmer Fudd's Sigfried. The cartoon suggests how our culture's conviction in high art was fading, as the opera was depicted without any serious dimension. We could argue that opera still had enough status that it could be the subject of parody. These cartoons were, after all, shown as shorts preceding movies in theaters, so the audiences would include adults, and *What's Opera, Doc?* still assumed the audience would know enough about opera to get the joke being had at the expense of this elite art. But the audience would not need any particular knowledge of Wagner to appreciate the humor of Elmer Fudd lisping dramatic arias.

Today's audience clicking through cartoons on YouTube thinks of opera in a different way, as representing the arbitrary taste of a small class of well-to-do people. In 1992, *What's Opera, Doc?* became the first cartoon short to be deemed "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant" by the United States Library of Congress and thus was selected for preservation in the National Film Registry (National Film Registry Web Site 2013). Elite status was being awarded to a popular cultural work that itself made fun of traditional elite culture.

Throughout the first half of the twentieth century, opera and symphonic music as well as live theatre were closely associated with economic and cultural elites in the United States, perhaps even more than in Europe. The association was not as close and restrictive in nineteenth-century America, as Laurence Levine showed in *High Brow, Low Brow* (1988). For much of the nineteenth century, Americans of all economic classes, at least in larger cities, might attend an evening of Shakespeare, presented by touring actors or a concert of operatic arias, perhaps even by a European star like Jenny Lind. Toward the end of the century, however, the cultural elites began to insist on "serious" productions and professional music-making, and Americans with less education and less money stopped attending. In the new century, they went to the movies instead. Until the middle of the twentieth century, stage plays, operas, and symphonic concerts belonged not exclusively but predominantly to the elites, until the notion of elite art began to break down, eroding in particular the cultural position of classical music. Theater fragmented into a variety of forms, some popular and some less so, but has obviously never regained the status it enjoyed before the coming of film.

A film from before the Second World War gives us another snapshot of the changing relationship of elite and popular in American culture: *The Philadelphia Story* (Cukor, 1940), based on an earlier play. The setting of this romantic comedy is the estate of the Lord family of the Philadelphia Mainline. Tracy Lord (Katherine Hepburn) and her ex-husband C.K. Dexter Haven (Cary Grant) belong to "high society," and Tracy must ultimately decide between Dexter-Haven and the reporter Macaulay Connor (James Stewart), a talented writer from the working class. Although it makes fun of the upper class's ostentation and its distance from working class concerns, the film revels visually in the wealth of Tracy and her relatives. Tracy may learn to appreciate the passion of life in a brief evening with Macauley, but she ultimately chooses to remarry Dexter-Haven and remain in her class.

For those who appreciate Hollywood's Golden Age, *The Philadelphia Story* with its charismatic stars and literate script is still a pleasure to watch. Its central problem, however, the problem of class in America, is framed in terms that are meaningless today. The film assumes that class is more than a matter of money---that it is still a valid, if somewhat unstable, social category. In 1940 it was possible to believe that class was ingrained if not hereditary and that high culture belonged to the upper class. Like *A Night at the Opera*, *The Philadelphia Story* can make fun of that idea, precisely because it is still current. For decades now, certainly since the 1960s, no one outside of those with inherited wealth believes in blue blood, at least in the United States, and probably increasingly in Europe. Certain art forms, such as opera and symphonic music, are associated with wealthy people, but these arts no longer validate the upper class. The tastes of certain social elites are now simply preferences, and the taste for traditionally elite art is not universal even among the economic elite. It no longer seems strange that a wealthy, educated man or woman might prefer country music to the operas that the Marx Brothers mocked.

If the reader is under forty, none of these references may mean much. Why worry about the fate of opera or symphonic music? But that is the point. If the idea of elite art were still powerful, then every educated individual would feel that she should know something about the operas of Mozart or the symphonies of Brahms. Living in the media plenitude means that there are no such required areas of cultural knowledge.
