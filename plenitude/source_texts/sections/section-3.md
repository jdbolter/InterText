## Class in America

In *The Philadelphia Story* and other comedies of that era, the "society column" is still an important feature of the newspaper. Today, where it still exists, it is an amusing relic. There are still debutante balls, but they have taken on the character of costume parties, performances that the culture at large regards as an earlier form of cosplay. *Socialite* is a term that designates a curious kind of celebrity, the kind possessed by Paris Hilton, who is notoriously famous for being famous. A broad public is still fascinated by the rich and famous, and the hereditary royal families of Europe are celebrities even in the United States. But on both sides of the Atlantic, society figures must share the pages of fan magazines with film stars, athletes, and rock stars. Rock and movie stars have become the model for the celebrity that royalty now share in, not the reverse. The phrase "rock star" (more than "king" or "queen") is now used to describe a person whose very appearance is an event. When in the 1950s, Grace Kelly gave up being a movie star to marry the Prince of Monaco, she moved up on the celebrity scale. By contrast, her daughter, Princess Stephanie, has a celebrity that is patterned on that of a fashion model (which she was) and a movie star, not the reverse. In the 1920s, Fitzgerald had said that the rich are different from us, and Hemingway had supposedly replied that yes, they have more money. Although the conversation is apocryphal, Hemingway's purported answer was the one that American culture came to adopt. There is no longer any widely shared belief that people in the upper class are essentially different from us. Class is as important as ever in American society, but it is understood exclusively in terms of money and the education and luxury that money can buy. When the *New York Times* published a book entitled *Class Matters* (2005), the reporters made it clear that money defines class. Being a member of the upper class is hereditary, but only in the sense that if your parents are rich and well educated, you are more likely to be both as well. It may be true that, as Tocqueville maintained, America has always been less disposed than Europe to the idea of an aristocracy defined by inherited qualities or simply birthright. But there was a sense of "proper people" that lingered in the United States in the first half of the twentieth century and faded remarkably quickly in the second half (Levine 1988; Beckert 2001).

The fading of this belief coincided with the loss of status for the arts associated with elites. If no group of people could convincingly claim a special status, no art forms could either. The operas that Tracy Lord and her family attended were not superior to popular music, just different. This does not mean that we have reached some utopian state of cultural equality. Our educational systems and social groups continue to harbor assumptions about the hierarchy of culture. But these assumptions are fragmentary, inconsistent, and not universally shared.
