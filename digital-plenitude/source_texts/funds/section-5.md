[burger-1984] Bürger, Theory of the Avant-Garde (1984). The historical avant-garde attacked art as an institution, not a rival style (pp. 20–22). Its shock was meant to jolt the public into changing how they lived, but shock gives no direction: the public answered the Dadaists with blind fury, and their lives probably did not change (p. 80). Shock also cannot last. Newspaper reports prepared audiences for the Dadaists, the public came expecting to be outraged, and the shock was "consumed" (pp. 80–81). When later artists repeated the gestures, the neo-avant-garde, they had lost their shock value and the institution of art absorbed them (pp. 57–58). Use: the theory behind the Zurich riot of 1919 and behind the later politeness of the public.

[macdonald-1962] Macdonald, "Masscult and Midcult" (1960; page numbers from its 1962 printing in Against the American Grain). By 1960 the bourgeoisie had developed a passion for being shocked: the more outrageous the artists, the more their middlebrow admirers were delighted (pp. 57–58). The spoofs of Dada had become the serious offerings of what he called a "lumpen-avant-garde" (p. 58). Use: a hostile witness to shock's domestication in the very years of Cage on television.

[greenberg-1939] Greenberg, "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" (1939). From a summary (cite only for these points): the avant-garde and kitsch arrived together, kitsch being commercial culture that loots a mature tradition's discoveries. Use: the critic who later defended Pollock's skill, in his earlier role as theorist of the divide.

[stubbs-2000] Stubbs, "Futurism and Surrealism: A Two-Speed Avant-Garde" (2000). Futurism and Surrealism share origins in Symbolism, Jarry and Lautréamont, and both set out to destroy the traditional conception of art in the name of life (pp. 305–306, 321). Use: on the Futurists and Surrealists.

[krauss-1981] Krauss, "The Originality of the Avant-Garde" (1981). Rodin's Gates of Hell, cast in 1978 under the terms of his will, is a legitimate original made sixty years after his death (pp. 47–48). The avant-garde's cult of originality rests on a repressed logic of the copy, which postmodern appropriation, such as Sherrie Levine's rephotographs, exposes (pp. 65–66). Use: on Duchamp's readymade and on what remained shocking once originality itself was in doubt.

[danto-1997] Danto, After the End of Art (1997). From a summary (cite only for these points): after the end of modernism's master narrative, anything can be art (p. 47); Pop transfigured the objects of ordinary experience into art (pp. 122, 130); painting became one medium among many (p. 136). Use: on Warhol's Brillo boxes and the end of formal shock.

[higgins-2007] Higgins, Horizons (1981 preamble). A Fluxus artist defends the avant-garde as the place where material determines form, and dismisses a "pop-garde" of fashionable but safe dissent (pp. 1–4 of the UbuWeb edition). Use: an artist's view from inside the avant-garde after its shock had faded.

[cesare-sandford-2010] Cesare and Sandford, "To Avant or Not to Avant" (TDR, 2010). New York's ensemble theatre after 2001 behaves like an avant-garde while the label no longer fits; the editors suggest that not being avant-garde may be the new avant-garde (p. 10). Use: on what became of shock in the twenty-first century.

[zryd-2006] Zryd, "The Academy and the Avant-Garde" (2006). From the abstract (cite only for this point): universities materially sustained North American avant-garde film in the 1970s and 1980s, with jobs, distribution, criticism and new audiences. Use: on how the avant-garde became one institutional community among others.

[edwards-2022] Edwards, "Fearing copyright issues, Getty Images bans AI-generated artwork" (Ars Technica, 2022). From the article's summary (cite only for this point): Getty excluded AI-generated images over unresolved rights. Use: a recent case where the new provocation is legal and economic, not formal.
