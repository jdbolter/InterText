[greenberg-1939] Greenberg, "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" (1939). One civilization produces at the same moment a poem by T. S. Eliot and a Tin Pan Alley song, and Greenberg asks what perspective can relate them. His answer divided culture in two: the avant-garde, and kitsch, the commercial art that loots a mature tradition and turns its discoveries into formula. Use: the classic statement of the divide that JAY-Z at the Pace Gallery no longer feels.

[macdonald-1962] Macdonald, "Masscult and Midcult" (1960; page numbers from its 1962 printing in Against the American Grain). Macdonald divided culture into High Culture and Masscult, which he called a parody of High Culture made for the market (p. 3). The more dangerous form was Midcult, which wears High Culture's standards while watering them down (pp. 37–40). His own remedy is close to the plenitude: the mass audience is divisible into many smaller audiences, and the more it is divided the better, though he wanted this to protect High Culture (pp. 72–74). Use: an opponent who described the fragmentation accurately and welcomed it for the opposite reason. Say so when citing him.

[huyssen-1986] Huyssen, After the Great Divide (1986). The phrase "the great divide" between modernism and mass culture is his. From a summary (cite only for these points): technology helped launch the avant-garde's break with tradition, yet it was the culture industry, not the avant-garde, that transformed everyday life in the twentieth century (p. 15). Use: the source of the name.

[levine-1988] Levine, Highbrow/Lowbrow (1988). From the publisher's description (cite only for this general point): the American categories of high and low, treated as natural, took shape only around the turn of the twentieth century, when Shakespeare and opera had earlier drawn audiences of every class.

[burger-1984] Bürger, Theory of the Avant-Garde (1984). After the historical avant-garde, the radically disparate exists side by side, and no movement can claim to be historically more advanced than any other (p. 63). Use: a theoretical statement, decades earlier, of the condition in which JAY-Z can list Rothko, Warhol and Basquiat without any order among them.

[bolter-2022] Bolter, "The Condition of Reading in a Digital Media Culture" (2022). Bolter's own later chapter, carrying this argument on. To its users the web looks flat, without centre or periphery, and it became a working image for a culture with no agreed centre and no single canon (pp. 38–39). Heterogeneity, not convergence on one medium, defines contemporary media culture (p. 36). Use: the argument's later development, cited by name and year.

[stiles-2025] Stiles, A LIVING POEM (Museum of Modern Art, 2025–2026). A poem co-written with an AI trained on the poet's own voice, rewriting itself every hour in MoMA's lobby (MoMA press release). Use: a recent case of the elite institution opening its doors to a popular, technological form, a successor to JAY-Z at Pace.

[chun-2021] Chun, Discriminating Data (2021). From the publisher's description (cite only for this general point): recommender systems group people with others like them, so that the clusters of the digital world are partly made by algorithms built on "homophily". Use: a challenge to the picture of communities as freely chosen.
