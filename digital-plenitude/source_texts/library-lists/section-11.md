[friedman-1998] Friedman, ed., The Fluxus Reader (1998). From the editor's introduction (cite only for these points): Fluxus is better understood as a set of ideas and a way of doing things than as a group; its twelve ideas include the unity of art and life, intermedia and playfulness. Use: Ono's Fluxus, and the avant-garde wish to bring art and life together.

[higgins-2007] Higgins, Horizons (1981 preamble). A Fluxus artist defends the avant-garde as the place where material determines form, and dismisses a "pop-garde" of fashionable but safe dissent (pp. 1–4 of the UbuWeb edition). Use: an avant-garde artist's suspicion of dissent made popular, as Lennon made it.

[burger-1984] Bürger, Theory of the Avant-Garde (1984). The avant-garde aimed to reintegrate art into the practice of life (pp. 49–54); the neo-avant-garde institutionalizes it as art and so negates its intentions (p. 58). Use: what Lennon carried into popular culture, and what it risked becoming there.

[macdonald-1962] Macdonald, "Masscult and Midcult" (1960; page numbers from its 1962 printing). By 1960 the bourgeoisie had developed a passion for being shocked: the more outrageous the artists, the more their middlebrow admirers were delighted (pp. 57–58). Use: an opponent's view of shock as a popular pleasure, as in the cover of Two Virgins.

[mcluhan-1964] McLuhan, Understanding Media (1964). Television, a "cool" medium, favoured performers who seemed casual and approachable (pp. 288–289). Use: McLuhan's own frame for the bed-in, a protest staged for television and the press.
