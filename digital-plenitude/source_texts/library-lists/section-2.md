[levine-1988] Levine, Highbrow/Lowbrow (1988). From the publisher's description (cite only for these points): the American categories of high and low, treated as natural, took shape only around the turn of the twentieth century; in the nineteenth century Shakespeare and opera drew audiences of every class; concert halls, opera houses and museums were then organised to serve separate, elite publics. The book itself calls him "Laurence Levine" and the book High Brow, Low Brow; the author is Lawrence W. Levine and the title Highbrow/Lowbrow. Use: the history behind the Philadelphia Orchestra's former place, and behind the audiences who left the theatre and the concert hall.

[macdonald-1962] Macdonald, "Masscult and Midcult" (1960; page numbers from its 1962 printing in Against the American Grain). Midcult pretends to respect High Culture's standards while watering them down (pp. 37–40). The postwar boom in orchestras, museums and art cinemas was real but passive, a matter of consuming rather than creating (pp. 58–61). Use: a hostile witness to popular culture approaching high culture on high culture's terms, as Fantasia did; say that he wrote as a defender of High Culture.

[wagner-1849] Wagner, The Art-Work of the Future (1849; translated by W. A. Ellis, 1895). The essay argues from the separation of the arts to their reunion in an artwork of the future. Use: the seriousness of the Wagnerian claim for opera, which What's Opera, Doc? deflates.

[pautz-2002] Pautz, "The Decline in Average Weekly Cinema Attendance: 1930–2000" (2002). Weekly cinema attendance in the United States fell from about 65 percent of the population in 1930 to under 10 percent in 2000 (p. 1), and television was the main substitute for going to the movies (pp. 1–2). Use: the audiences who left the theatre and the concert hall for the movies, and later left the movies for television.

[hirsch-1987] Hirsch, Kett and Trefil, Cultural Literacy (1987). From the publisher's account (cite only for these points): reading depends on shared background knowledge, and schools should teach a common core of information; the book lists nearly 5,000 names, dates, phrases and texts that literate Americans were said to know. Use: the "required areas of cultural knowledge" that no longer exist.

[bourdieu-1984] Bourdieu, Distinction (1984). From the publisher's description (cite only for this point): judgements of taste are socially structured, and distinctions of taste reproduce distinctions of class. Use: opera as the mark of a class, as it was becoming by the Depression; the plenitude's argument is that cultural and economic elites have since parted company.

[greenberg-1939] Greenberg, "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" (1939). One civilization produces at the same moment a poem by T. S. Eliot and a Tin Pan Alley song; Greenberg divided culture into the avant-garde and kitsch, the commercial art that loots a mature tradition and turns its discoveries into formula. Use: how a critic of 1939 would have placed Fantasia's borrowing of Bach and Stravinsky.
