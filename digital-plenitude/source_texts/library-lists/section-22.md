[henrickson-2025] Henrickson, Digital Storytelling (2025). Following Marie-Laure Ryan, interactivity can be internal or external to the story world, and exploratory or ontological (changing what happens) (p. 83); in procedural rhetoric, meaning lies in what the player can, cannot and must do (pp. 84–85). Use: why the interaction in cathartic games is largely exploratory, a way of uncovering a story already told, and has little to do with the emotion.

[mateas-stern-2005] Mateas and Stern, Façade (2005). From a description of the work (cite only for these points): the player converses with a married couple in typed language, and a drama manager orders the story's events toward tension and resolution, with an outcome that varies from play to play. Use: an attempt to make the interaction itself carry the drama, the hard case the section says rarely succeeds.

[murray-1997] Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck (1997). From the author's notes: immersion, agency and transformation are the digital medium's characteristic pleasures. Use: agency, the pleasure that cathartic games largely give up in favour of immersion in a story.

[schrank-2014] Schrank, Avant-Garde Videogames (2014). From the abstract (cite only for these points): a mainstream game keeps the player in a closed circuit; an avant-garde game opens the circuit and draws attention to itself as a game. Use: the games at the other end from Edith Finch, which refuse absorption altogether.

[flanagan-2009] Flanagan, Critical Play (2009). From the preface and contents (cite only for this point): play can be a mode of critique, and games can be designed for it. Use: critical play as the Brechtian alternative to cathartic games.

[wu-2025] Wu and colleagues, "Towards Enhanced Immersion and Agency for LLM-based Interactive Drama" (2025). From the abstract (cite only for these points): immersion and agency are the qualities that make interactive drama with language models enjoyable; playwriting guidance and plot-based reflection improved both, as judged by human readers. Use: the newest attempt to combine interaction and dramatic storytelling, now with language models.

[morganti-2013] Morganti, "Gone Home" (review, 2013). From the work's own use: "Many people read books for the opportunity to see life through someone else's eyes, but it's rare for a game to do it as well as this one does." Use: Gone Home as a game that reads like fiction.

[webster-2017] Webster, "What Remains of Edith Finch Is the Game Equivalent of a Haunting Short Story Collection" (2017). From the work's own use: "Every story comes around to the same conclusion: death. So why did I often [find] myself shocked when the moment came? I suppose I regretted having to let go of one character—and the world—to hurry on to the next." Use: the cathartic effect of Edith Finch, in a reviewer's words.

[moriarty-2013] Moriarty, "The Last of Us" (review, 2013). From the work's own use (cite only for this point): the review compares The Last of Us explicitly to Cormac McCarthy's novel The Road. Use: a violent mainstream game read as a novel.

[ryan-2006] Ryan, Avatars of Story (2006). From the work's own use, and Henrickson's summary (cite only for these points): interactivity and emotionally engaging, coherent storytelling are difficult to combine; interactivity can be internal or external to the story world, exploratory or ontological (pp. 107–122). Use: why cathartic games remain a limited genre.

[bogost-2017] Bogost, "Video Games Are Better without Stories" (2017). From the work's own use (cite only for this point): video games should simply leave storytelling to novels, film and television. Use: a game scholar's case against storytelling in games.
