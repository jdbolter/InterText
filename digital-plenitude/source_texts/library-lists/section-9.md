[greenberg-1960] Greenberg, "Modernist Painting" (1960). From a summary (cite only for these points): modernism is self-criticism on Kant's model; each art secures its standards by purging effects borrowed from the other arts, so that its uniqueness lies in its medium; for painting, that meant flatness. Use: formal modernism, and the idea of medium specificity.

[greenberg-1939] Greenberg, "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" (1939). From a summary (cite only for these points): the avant-garde and kitsch arrived together, kitsch being commercial culture that loots a mature tradition's discoveries. Use: the moment when all modernism counted as avant-garde against nineteenth-century mainstream art.

[danto-1997] Danto, After the End of Art (1997). Greenberg is the great narrator of modernism, belonging to the age of manifestos in seeking a definition of the essence of painting (pp. 8, 68–69). Use: on formal modernism and the critic who told its story.

[burger-1984] Bürger, Theory of the Avant-Garde (1984). The historical avant-garde attacked art as an institution, not a rival style (pp. 20–22), and aimed to reintegrate art into the practice of life; the culture industry offers a false version of that integration, in pulp fiction and commodity aesthetics (pp. 49–54). The neo-avant-garde institutionalizes the avant-garde as art and so negates its intentions (p. 58). Use: the avant-garde strand, and what became of it.

[schrank-2014] Schrank, Avant-Garde Videogames (2014). From the abstract (cite only for these points): avant-garde games can be judged by how they open the experience of games (formal) or of being in the world (political), in four varieties, radical or complicit, formal or political. Use: the work's own acknowledged debt, a closely related distinction between formal and political avant-gardes, applied to video games.

[stubbs-2000] Stubbs, "Futurism and Surrealism: A Two-Speed Avant-Garde" (2000). Futurism and Surrealism share origins in Symbolism, Jarry and Lautréamont, and both set out to destroy the traditional conception of art in the name of life (pp. 305–306, 321). Use: on the Futurists and Surrealists.
