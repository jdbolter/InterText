[lessig-2008] Lessig, Remix (2008). Digital technologies make a "Read/Write" culture possible again (pp. 33–35), and remix, especially of borrowed video with recorded music, has become a means of expression for a large population of young amateurs. Use: remix as a practice of millions, not only of professionals.

[lessig-2007] Lessig, "Laws That Choke Creativity" (TED talk, 2007). From the work's own use: "anybody with access to a $1500 computer … can take sounds and images from the culture around us and use it to say things differently." Use: the cheap tools that opened remix to amateurs.

[navas-2015] Navas, Gallagher and burrough, eds., The Routledge Companion to Remix Studies (2015). From the work's own use (cite only for this point): the practice of sampling and recombining now extends from music to video, still images and text, and has become a field of study. Use: the reach of remix beyond music.

[shifman-2014] Shifman, Memes in Digital Culture (2014). From the work's own use: Richard Dawkins coined the meme in 1976 for cultural units that spread from person to person (p. 2); a meme is constituted by repetition and variation, and a video becomes a meme only when many others imitate it (p. 55). Use: the Downfall parodies as a meme, and remix as the way memes spread.

[jenkins-1992] Jenkins, Textual Poachers (1992). From the work's own use (cite only for this point): fans of popular series passed easily from consumers to producers of media, writing fan fiction that extends the storyworlds they love. Use: video remix as a kind of fan fiction, appropriating a small part of Hollywood's world.

[jenkins-2006] Jenkins, Convergence Culture (2006). From the publisher's description (cite only for these points): convergence is cultural as much as technical, and corporate and grassroots media sometimes cooperate and sometimes conflict. Use: the participatory culture in which amateur remix flourishes, and its friction with the studios.
